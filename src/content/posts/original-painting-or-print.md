---
title: How to Tell an Original Painting from a Print
description: A few practical checks any collector can make with a good light and a magnifier before buying a picture sold as an original.
pubDate: 2026-11-02
---

A framed picture can look convincing from across the room. Up close, a few simple checks will usually tell you whether you are looking at paint applied by hand or an image printed onto paper or canvas. None of them need special training. A strong light and an inexpensive magnifying loupe go a long way.

## Look at the surface from an angle

Hold a light low and to one side, then look across the picture rather than straight at it. Oil and acrylic paint sit on the surface with real thickness. You will often see ridges from the brush, places where paint was built up, and areas where the canvas weave shows through thinner passages. A print is usually flat and even. Some reproductions add clear brushstroke texture on top, but that texture rarely lines up with the shapes and edges in the image underneath.

## Use a magnifier

Under a loupe, most printed images break down into a regular pattern of tiny dots or a very fine, uniform grain. Paint does not. In a watercolor you should see pigment pooling and soaking into the paper fibers unevenly. In an oil, colors blend and overlap where the brush moved them.

## Check the edges

Look where the image meets the margin or the tacking edge of the canvas. Painters often leave uneven edges, drips, or bare canvas around the sides. A print tends to stop at a clean, machine-straight border, or the image may wrap around the stretcher in a way that looks too tidy.

## Turn it over

The back of a picture can tell you a lot. Look at the stretcher bars or backing, any labels, stamps, or handwriting, and the condition of the canvas or paper. Age, framing history, and gallery or exhibition labels are all worth noting, though labels can be moved from one picture to another, so treat them as clues rather than proof.

## Read the signature carefully

A hand-painted signature sits in the paint layer and may show the same texture as the rest of the work. A printed signature is flat like the rest of the image. A pencil signature on a print may mean a signed limited edition, which is a legitimate work in its own right, but it is not a painting.

## Ask the seller

A reputable dealer should tell you plainly what a work is, what medium it is in, and what is and is not known about its history. If something is uncertain, the description should say so. If you are unsure about a picture, ask questions before you buy, and ask for clear photographs of the surface and the back.
