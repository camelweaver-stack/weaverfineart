Richard Earlom scraped the mezzotints for John Boydell's Houghton Gallery, the print project after the pictures at Houghton Hall in Norfolk. The two plates after Jan van Huysum are [A Flower Piece](/works/richard-earlom-a-flower-piece/), lettered 25 June 1778, and [A Fruit Piece](/works/richard-earlom-a-fruit-piece/), whose own publication line reads "Published Sept. 1st, 1781 by John Boydell Engraver in Cheapside London". Some catalogues cite 1 November 1781 for other states of the Fruit Piece. Joseph Farington, with others, made the drawings. Earlom engraved after those drawings.

**The paintings and the 1779 sale.** Van Huysum painted the Flower Piece in 1722 and the Fruit Piece in 1723. Both hung in Sir Robert Walpole's collection at Houghton Hall, one of the finest in Britain. In 1779 Walpole's grandson, George, 3rd Earl of Orford, sold the greater part of that collection to Catherine II of Russia. The two paintings went to St Petersburg and remain in the State Hermitage Museum. Boydell had already begun recording the collection in prints. Walpole and, after the sale, Catherine the Great are the notable historical owners of the paintings. Boydell is the publisher of the plates.

**Boydell's Houghton Gallery.** The plates were issued in batches of ten between 1774 and 1788. On 1 January 1788 John and Josiah Boydell published the collected work in two elephant-folio volumes, dedicated to Catherine II, under the title *A Set of Prints Engraved after the Most Capital Paintings in the Collection of Her Imperial Majesty, the Empress of Russia, Lately in the Possession of the Earl of Orford at Houghton in Norfolk*. Plates keep their earlier publication dates. The 1788 title pages do not mean the Flower and Fruit plates were first printed in 1788. The [Royal Academy's set](https://www.royalacademy.org.uk/art-artists/book/a-set-of-prints-engraved-after-the-most-capital-paintings-in-the-collection) describes 129 pictures, with the van Huysums as volume I, plates 59 and 60, plus plans and elevations. The Art Institute of Chicago describes the finished book as 162 prints, including 27 by Earlom. An [Eton College copy](https://catalogue.etoncollege.com/B32188) has 162 plates on 133 leaves. Those counts agree if one is pictures only and the other includes frontispieces, plans and titles.

**Popularity and the second plates.** Boydell's table of contents, quoted on the Smithsonian's impressions from George P. Marsh's copy of volume I, said the flower and fruit plates surpassed, in point of execution, every print before engraved in this manner. They were among the most admired plates in the set and were also sold as a pair. Demand was high enough that they were re-engraved. A [British Museum curator's note](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/P_1838-0420-206), citing Duchesne's 1823 account, says the second version was judged inferior to the first. Wessely numbered the first plates 144 and 145 and the later plates 146 and 147. [Harvard holds a Fruit Piece catalogued as Wessely 147](https://harvardartmuseums.org/collections/object/275220). The motto *Fari quae sentiat* on the Walpole arms is not, by itself, enough to tell a late state of the first plates from the re-engraved plates.

**Why early impressions are prized.** The burr that gives a mezzotint its rich black wears down as the plate is printed, so an early proof is deeper than a late pull. Proofs before the letters, and impressions taken before the motto was added to the arms, are scarcer than fully lettered sheets and appear less often. The two impressions offered here are fully lettered published states, with Boydell's Cheapside address and the Walpole arms. Their color is later hand-coloring, not color from the time of printing. They are not proofs, not unique, and the dates in the plates do not prove they were pulled in 1778 or 1781. Those dates remained on the copper when impressions were printed for the 1788 volumes. Which plate, the first or the re-engraved one, cannot be decided from the framed sheets.

**Impressions in public collections.** No full census exists. These are impressions confirmed from official records. Bound sets at the Royal Academy and at Eton still contain the plates. Other volumes were broken up: the Smithsonian's Flower Piece was removed from Marsh's volume for an 1888 exhibition, and the Fruit Piece was removed for framing in 1894. Loose sheets then entered the decorative-print market, where later hand-coloring and gilt frames are ordinary.
